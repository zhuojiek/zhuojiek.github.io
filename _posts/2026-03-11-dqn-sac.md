[Policy gradients](/posts/policy-gradients) throw away every sample after one update. Value-based methods keep a replay buffer and regress a Q-function onto bootstrapped targets, which is far more sample efficient. The price is that you are now training a network on its own predictions. Most of this post is about the ways that goes wrong and the small fixes that stop it.

## DQN

The Q-learning target is $$y = r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q_{\bar\phi}(s', a')$$, where $$\bar\phi$$ is a slowly updated target network. On CartPole, plain DQN learns quickly and then won't settle down:

<figure class="half">
  <img src="/images/posts/dqn-sac/dqn-cartpole.png" alt="DQN on CartPole: eval return spikes to 500 several times but oscillates between 100 and 500.">
  <img src="/images/posts/dqn-sac/ddqn-lunar.png" alt="Double DQN on LunarLander: eval return climbs from about -1400 to about 220 by 250k steps and stays there.">
  <figcaption>Left: DQN on CartPole-v1 hits the maximum of 500 three times and falls off every time. Right: double DQN on LunarLander-v2 settles around +220.</figcaption>
</figure>

The CartPole curve is the classic deadly triad<span class="sidenote">Sutton & Barto, <em>Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction</em>, §11.3.</span>: function approximation, bootstrapping, and off-policy data. Every Q update moves the targets of every other state. The $$\max$$ makes it worse, because it picks whichever action's estimate happens to be most positively wrong. That is a systematic overestimation that bootstrapping then compounds. **Double DQN** decouples choosing the action from evaluating it, using $$y = r + \gamma\, Q_{\bar\phi}(s', \arg\max_{a'} Q_\phi(s', a'))$$. The online network picks the action and the target network scores it, so the two have to be wrong in the same direction for the error to propagate.

The train/eval gap on the right comes from exploration, not overfitting. Training uses ε-greedy, and early on ε is high enough that random actions keep crashing the lander. Evaluation is fully greedy, so it shows what the Q-function actually knows.

I also started a learning-rate sweep on LunarLander, but the non-default runs only went about 40k steps. Over that range 1e-2 through 1e-4 look the same, and that isn't long enough to conclude anything.

## SAC

For continuous actions you can't take a $$\max$$ over $$a'$$, so you learn a policy that does the maximizing. Soft actor-critic adds an entropy bonus to the objective, so the critic learns soft Q-values:

$$y = r + \gamma\Big(\min_{j=1,2} Q_{\bar\phi_j}(s', a') - \alpha \log \pi(a' \mid s')\Big), \quad a' \sim \pi(\cdot \mid s'),$$

and the actor minimizes $$\mathbb{E}\big[\alpha \log\pi(a \mid s) - Q(s, a)\big]$$ with reparameterized samples $$a = \tanh(\mu_\theta(s) + \sigma_\theta(s)\,\epsilon)$$.

### Clipped double-Q

The $$\min$$ over two critics in the target is SAC's version of the double DQN fix. Ablating it on Hopper made the biggest difference of anything in this homework:

<figure>
  <img src="/images/posts/dqn-sac/sac-clipped-q.png" alt="Hopper: clipped double-Q reaches 2500-3200 return while single-Q stays around 750; single-Q's Q-values are higher for the first 300k steps." style="max-width:620px">
  <figcaption>Top: return. Bottom: the critic's Q estimates on sampled batches.</figcaption>
</figure>

With a single critic, Hopper plateaus around 750. With clipped double-Q it reaches 2500–3200. The bottom plot is the interesting one. For the first 300k steps the single critic reports *higher* Q-values than the clipped one, even though its policy is far worse. Those are overestimates. The actor climbs the critic's mistakes, finds actions whose values are inflated, and reinforces them. Taking the minimum of two independently initialized critics is a cheap pessimism that breaks that loop.

### Auto-tuned temperature

Instead of fixing $$\alpha$$, SAC can treat it as a Lagrange multiplier on an entropy constraint $$\mathbb{E}[-\log\pi] \ge \bar{\mathcal{H}}$$ (the target is usually $$-\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert$$) and learn it by gradient descent on $$\alpha\,(-\log\pi - \bar{\mathcal{H}})$$.

<figure>
  <img src="/images/posts/dqn-sac/sac-temperature.png" alt="Top: auto-tuned temperature reaches about 7000 on HalfCheetah by 375k steps vs about 5100 for fixed. Bottom: learned alpha drops from 0.1 to 0.02 by 30k steps, then rises steadily back to about 0.1." style="max-width:620px">
  <figcaption>HalfCheetah. Top: fixed vs. auto-tuned \(\alpha\). Bottom: the learned \(\alpha\).</figcaption>
</figure>

I have to correct my homework writeup here. I wrote that auto-tuning "achieves comparable performance" and that the temperature "decreases over training and then stabilizes". The plot says neither. Auto-tuning is clearly better, about 7000 vs. 5100 at the same step count. And $$\alpha$$ does not settle. It crashes from 0.1 to 0.02 in the first 30k steps, then climbs steadily back to about 0.1 for the rest of training.

The constraint explains the shape:

1. **Early:** the policy is close to its initialization, so its entropy is well above the target. The constraint is slack, and gradient descent pushes $$\alpha$$ down fast. That is the first 30k steps.
2. **Later:** as the critic learns, Q-values grow (this is a dense-reward task with returns in the thousands), and the gradient of $$-Q$$ increasingly dominates the actor loss. The policy sharpens, its entropy drops *below* the target, and $$\alpha$$ has to rise to keep it there.

So the rising $$\alpha$$ doesn't mean the agent "wants to explore more" late in training. It means the controller is pushing back against a policy that is collapsing because the reward scale keeps growing. A fixed $$\alpha$$ doesn't do that, which is probably why the fixed run is slower: its effective entropy regularization fades as the Q-values grow.

All of this is online, though: the agent collects its own data. When it can't, [the critic's mistakes get much worse](/posts/offline-rl).
