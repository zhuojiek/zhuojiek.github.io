[Policy gradients](/posts/policy-gradients) use each sample for one update and then discard it. Value-based methods keep a replay buffer and regress a Q-function onto bootstrapped targets, which is much more sample efficient, but the network is trained on its own predictions. This post covers the problems that causes and the fixes DQN and SAC use.

## DQN

The Q-learning target is $$y = r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q_{\bar\phi}(s', a')$$, where $$\bar\phi$$ is a slowly updated target network. On CartPole, plain DQN learns quickly but doesn't stay stable:

<figure class="half">
  <img src="/images/posts/dqn-sac/dqn-cartpole.png" alt="DQN on CartPole: eval return spikes to 500 several times but oscillates between 100 and 500.">
  <img src="/images/posts/dqn-sac/ddqn-lunar.png" alt="Double DQN on LunarLander: eval return climbs from about -1400 to about 220 by 250k steps and stays there.">
  <figcaption>Left: DQN on CartPole-v1 hits the maximum of 500 three times and falls off every time. Right: double DQN on LunarLander-v2 settles around +220.</figcaption>
</figure>

The CartPole instability is an example of the deadly triad<span class="sidenote">Sutton & Barto, <em>Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction</em>, §11.3.</span>: function approximation, bootstrapping, and off-policy data. Every Q update moves the targets of every other state. The $$\max$$ makes this worse because it selects the action whose estimate is most overestimated, and bootstrapping compounds that bias. **Double DQN** decouples choosing the action from evaluating it, using $$y = r + \gamma\, Q_{\bar\phi}(s', \arg\max_{a'} Q_\phi(s', a'))$$. The online network picks the action and the target network scores it, so the two have to be wrong in the same direction for the error to propagate.

The train/eval gap on the right comes from exploration, not overfitting. Training uses ε-greedy, and early on ε is high enough that random actions keep crashing the lander. Evaluation is fully greedy, so it reflects the greedy policy of the learned Q-function.

I also started a learning-rate sweep on LunarLander, but the non-default runs only went about 40k steps. Over that range 1e-2 through 1e-4 look the same, which is too short to draw conclusions.

## SAC

For continuous actions you can't take a $$\max$$ over $$a'$$, so you learn a policy that does the maximizing. Soft actor-critic adds an entropy bonus to the objective, so the critic learns soft Q-values:

$$y = r + \gamma\Big(\min_{j=1,2} Q_{\bar\phi_j}(s', a') - \alpha \log \pi(a' \mid s')\Big), \quad a' \sim \pi(\cdot \mid s'),$$

and the actor minimizes $$\mathbb{E}\big[\alpha \log\pi(a \mid s) - Q(s, a)\big]$$ with reparameterized samples $$a = \tanh(\mu_\theta(s) + \sigma_\theta(s)\,\epsilon)$$.

### Clipped double-Q

The $$\min$$ over two critics in the target is SAC's version of the double DQN fix. Removing it on Hopper had the largest effect of any change in this homework:

<figure>
  <img src="/images/posts/dqn-sac/sac-clipped-q.png" alt="Hopper: clipped double-Q reaches 2500-3200 return while single-Q stays around 750; single-Q's Q-values are higher for the first 300k steps." style="max-width:620px">
  <figcaption>Top: return. Bottom: the critic's Q estimates on sampled batches.</figcaption>
</figure>

With a single critic, Hopper plateaus around 750. With clipped double-Q it reaches 2500–3200. In the bottom plot, the single critic reports higher Q-values than the clipped one for the first 300k steps, even though its policy is much worse, so those values are overestimates. The actor moves toward actions whose values are inflated, which reinforces the error. Taking the minimum of two independently initialized critics is a simple form of pessimism that counters this.

### Auto-tuned temperature

Instead of fixing $$\alpha$$, SAC can treat it as a Lagrange multiplier on an entropy constraint $$\mathbb{E}[-\log\pi] \ge \bar{\mathcal{H}}$$ (the target is usually $$-\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert$$) and learn it by gradient descent on $$\alpha\,(-\log\pi - \bar{\mathcal{H}})$$.

<figure>
  <img src="/images/posts/dqn-sac/sac-temperature.png" alt="Top: auto-tuned temperature reaches about 7000 on HalfCheetah by 375k steps vs about 5100 for fixed. Bottom: learned alpha drops from 0.1 to 0.02 by 30k steps, then rises steadily back to about 0.1." style="max-width:620px">
  <figcaption>HalfCheetah. Top: fixed vs. auto-tuned \(\alpha\). Bottom: the learned \(\alpha\).</figcaption>
</figure>

This plot contradicts my homework writeup, where I wrote that auto-tuning "achieves comparable performance" and that the temperature "decreases over training and then stabilizes". Auto-tuning does better, about 7000 vs. 5100 at the same step count, and $$\alpha$$ does not stabilize. It drops from 0.1 to 0.02 in the first 30k steps, then rises steadily back to about 0.1 for the rest of training.

The entropy constraint explains this:

1. **Early:** the policy is close to its initialization, so its entropy is well above the target. The constraint is slack, so gradient descent lowers $$\alpha$$ quickly over the first 30k steps.
2. **Later:** as the critic learns, Q-values grow (this is a dense-reward task with returns in the thousands), and the gradient of $$-Q$$ increasingly dominates the actor loss. The policy sharpens, its entropy drops *below* the target, and $$\alpha$$ rises to push it back up.

The rising $$\alpha$$ compensates for the growing reward scale, which would otherwise make the policy's entropy collapse. With a fixed $$\alpha$$, the entropy term becomes relatively weaker as Q-values grow, which is probably why the fixed run learns more slowly.

All of this is online, where the agent collects its own data. Without that, [the critic's errors are harder to correct](/posts/offline-rl).
